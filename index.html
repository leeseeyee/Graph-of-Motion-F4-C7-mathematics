Case Study · Instructional Design

Teaching that a graph is not a map.

An interactive learning object that targets one specific, stubborn misconception in Form 4 Mathematics — and is built to check whether the misconception actually shifted.

Role
Instructional Designer — concept, design & build
Subject
Form 4 Maths · Ch.7 Graphs of Motion
Learners
~16-year-old students
Format
Interactive object + diagnostic quiz
Built with
HTML / CSS / JS · ADDIE
1 The problem

Students read the line as the road.

Ask a Form 4 class to describe the journey on a distance–time graph and a predictable thing happens: many describe the shape of the line as if it were the road itself. A line sloping up becomes "driving uphill"; a line sloping down becomes "going downhill" or "going faster".

The root issue isn't carelessness — it's a mental model. Students treat the graph as a picture of the path, not as a record of distance from the start over time. Until that model is corrected, the core of Chapter 7 — gradient as speed, area as distance — never quite lands.

So I stopped thinking of it as "a lesson on graphs of motion" and started thinking of it as going after one specific wrong idea — and the rest of the design followed from that.

2 Design approach

Every decision exists to make the wrong model visible — then break it.

My first instinct was to just embed an existing GeoGebra simulation — there are good ones, and I’d used one in my own coursework. But that’s exactly where the problem showed up: it was too open. Students dragged the sliders, made a nice-looking graph, and never actually ran into the misconception. An open sandbox lets you slide right past the hard idea.

So instead of a free playground, I built something narrower — every scenario is chosen to put the wrong model under pressure. I worked through ADDIE, but a few decisions did the real heavy lifting:

A flat, straight track — on purpose

The car only ever moves left–right, but the graph moves up and down. That contradiction is the whole point.

Two graphs, drawn live

d–t and v–t build in real time as the car moves, so students meet gradient-as-speed and area-as-distance attached to something moving — not as separate formulas.

Feedback the moment it goes wrong

When the car reverses, the line falls — the exact spot students say "going down". A prompt calls out the trap right there, not afterward.

A view of the mistake itself

One toggle shows what the graph would mean if it really were a map: the car lifts off the ground and flies. Letting the wrong idea fail does more than telling students it's wrong.

Formulas tied to live numbers

The Chapter 7 relationships update as the car moves, so the symbols stay attached to something the student can see.

A quiz where the wrong answers are the misconceptions

Each distractor is a specific error students actually make — so a wrong answer tells me which idea to fix, not just that they missed it.

3 The solution

Drive it yourself.

The learner picks a journey, presses play, and watches the car and both graphs move together. The strongest moment is "There & back": the car returns to start, the line clearly falls, and the live prompt catches the instinct to say "going down".

4 How I measure it

The quiz is data, not just a score.

Because each wrong answer maps to a named misconception, a class set of responses becomes diagnostic. It tells a teacher which wrong model is most common — and therefore exactly what to reteach.

If a student picks…
…it reveals this misconception
"Driving downhill" (line falls)
Reads the graph as the road
"Steady speed" (flat line)
Confuses "no movement" with "constant speed"
"Reversing" (v–t falls, stays positive)
Confuses decelerating with going backward

I turned that into a learning-analytics dashboard: it aggregates the quiz responses and flags the most common misconception per class, then recommends what to reteach first. Switch between classes below — the diagnosis and the recommended action change with the data.

5 Why the method transfers

It's a maths topic. The method isn't.

The subject here is school mathematics, but the design method is subject-agnostic — the same five steps work for compliance training, software onboarding, or sales upskilling, anywhere learners hold a confident-but-wrong mental model:

  1. Identify a specific learner misconception — not a topic.
  2. Design an interactive that makes the wrong model visible.
  3. Give feedback at the exact point of error.
  4. Measure with distractors that diagnose, not just score.
  5. Surface the pattern to the facilitator as analytics.
